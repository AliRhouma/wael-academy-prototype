Critique of the feature plan: coins, the town, community, analytics and AI

This review looks at whether the plan helps Tunisian secondary and bac students learn more, keeps them coming back for healthy reasons, holds together economically, and is safe for minors and acceptable to the app stores. It covers each area in turn and ends with a recommended order of work and the open questions the client needs to answer.

Prepared 5 November 2026 · Based on the client's plan summary and the clickable prototype (student space /student-v2, seed data, gamified-skin brief) · Not legal advice

The five points that matter most

  1. Build the practice engine first, because everything else depends on it. Points, leagues, live rooms, diagnosis, recommendation and AI generation all need the same thing: a bank of practice questions, each tagged by chapter, skill and difficulty, answered in study mode with immediate feedback and brought back later for review. Today that is listed as "upcoming". It should be phase 1, and every reward should hang off a verified step in it.
  2. Points must reward evidence of learning, never time spent. Minutes of video, PDF downloads and idle attendance are easy to farm (muted tabs, a phone left playing). Playback at 2× is not the problem: studies find no loss in comprehension up to 2×[7]. The problem is paying out for presence. Award points for correct first attempts, for getting an item right again days later, and for check questions inside videos. Students who "game the system" learn measurably less[12].
  3. Redesign coins before building them. Keep coins earned-only in version 1. Unlock whole chapters rather than single files, and keep unlocks until the end of the school year so students can review before exams. Drop "rate the app for coins": Google Play forbids incentivised ratings[22] and Apple requires its own review prompt[20]. Selling coins for real money triggers mandatory in-app purchase and store commission[20][21], and many Tunisian bank cards can't pay in foreign currency[26].
  4. Minors change the rules: involve parents and plan for compliance from day one. Many users are minors, and the prototype's offers start at 6th year primary (about age 11). Tunisian Law 2004-63 requires the guardian's consent and the family judge's authorisation to process a child's data (art. 28), a prior declaration to the INPDP, the national data-protection authority (art. 7), and INPDP authorisation for any transfer abroad (art. 52). That covers foreign analytics, realtime and AI vendors[24]. Chat requires filtering, reporting and blocking[20]. The plan never mentions parents, even though they are a role in the product, the payers and the legal gatekeepers.
  5. Keep the good part of Clash of Clans and leave the engine behind. Make the town a map of mastery: each building is a subject and each floor is a chapter the student has mastered and re-checked. Replace the global league with small weekly cohorts of similar level, use flexible weekly goals instead of streaks, and never use random rewards or paid entry. Extrinsic rewards that are tangible and expected undermine intrinsic motivation[8], and leaderboards and badges have been shown to lower motivation over a school term[10].

AThe plan as a whole

Good but can be enhanced

The direction is right: practice-centred content, learning-driven progress and AI feedback. But it is five products at once, it leans on rewards more than on learning, and it ignores two of the product's four roles.

What to keep

KeepSeparate currencies for "progress" (points) and "spending" (coins)

This is the most important structural choice in the plan, and it is correct. Progress that can be bought stops meaning anything. Keep the wall between the two absolute: points never convert into coins.

KeepPoints are earned from learning, and some buildings only accept points from a specific subject

This turns balance between subjects into something the student can see, which is a real learning nudge. Many bac students neglect their weak subject.

KeepGoing from diagnosis to recommendation to a plan

Closing the loop from "what I don't know" to "what I do on Tuesday" is what most edtech never ships, and it is what bac students struggle with most.

Critiques

C1Five products at once

A virtual-goods economy with an e-store and cash on delivery, a base-building game, realtime multiplayer with chat, a recommender, and AI vision grading. Each of these has its own team at companies that ship only that one thing. Built in parallel, each will be shallow, and the parts will disagree about what "progress" means.

C2The learning core is the dependency, and it is the least specified part

Quizzes, exercises in study mode and exams are "upcoming". Yet points need graded attempts, leagues need points, live rooms need question series, diagnosis needs questions mapped to skills, recommendation needs diagnosis, and AI generation needs a model of what a good exercise looks like. The plan spends most of its words on rewards around learning and one line on learning itself.

C3Too many extrinsic reward layers

Coins, points, a league, tiers, badges, and physical goods bought with coins. A meta-analysis of 128 experiments found that rewards which are tangible, expected and tied to the task undermine intrinsic motivation[8]. Gamification in education has small positive average effects, but the results vary widely and depend on design[9]. In a semester-long classroom study, leaderboards and badges lowered motivation, satisfaction and empowerment compared with a control group, and that drop carried through to exam scores[10]. More layers does not mean more learning.

C4Parents and teachers are absent

The product has four roles, but the plan only addresses students and admin. Parents are the payers, the legal consent-givers for minors, and the people who receive cash-on-delivery parcels. Teachers are the source of quality, live sessions and moderation. Both are untapped levers. See Parents & teachers.

C5It reverses the current design brief without saying so

The repo's gamified-skin brief (.claude/skills/wael-gamified, docs/mobile/gamification-design.md) currently says "No XP, points, levels, leagues, streaks, hearts or energy; completion only from real or session data", with a principle of "never nagging, never fake progress". Adding points and leagues is a legitimate product decision. It should be taken deliberately, keeping that brief's guardrails.

C6Compliance with rules for minors is not considered

Ads aimed at minors, chat between minors, behavioural profiling, photos of minors' work sent to AI vendors, and minors placing cash-on-delivery orders each bring legal and store-policy obligations. They are cheaper to design in than to retrofit.

Propositions

P1Write down the core learning loop, then attach every feature to one of its steps

LearnLive session, recording, video, PDF
→
PractiseRetrieval questions in study mode, immediate explained feedback
→
Review laterMissed items come back after days (spaced, interleaved)
→
See masteryChapter mastered, town floor built, parent and teacher informed

Retrieval practice (testing yourself) and spaced practice are the two study techniques with the strongest evidence. Rereading and highlighting are among the weakest[1][2][3].

Why it helps: The product's centre of gravity moves from consuming content (the current dashboard of videos and PDFs) to practising and recalling, which is where learning actually happens. Every reward then celebrates something real.

P2One "mastery" truth that every feature reads

Compute one mastery state per student per chapter from practice results, for example "not started, practising, mastered, re-checked". The town, league, recommendations, planner, parent digest and teacher heatmap all read this single value.

Why it helps: No feature can disagree with another about progress. It also makes fake progress structurally impossible.

P3Adopt a one-page guardrail charter before building

  • No random or paid rewards (no chests, spin wheels or loot boxes). Loot-box spending is linked to problem gambling[14].
  • No paid entry to any competition. No streak-loss theatre and no guilt notifications.
  • No ads inside study, exam or video flows. Ads only at natural pauses, with a daily cap.
  • Points come only from verified learning. Progress can never be bought.
  • For younger students, no public ranking by real name and no free-text chat by default.
  • Parents can see, and switch off, social and spending features.

1Coins economy & Wael Library

Weak (as specified)

The intent is good: an entry path for families who can't pay for an annual offer. As described, though, it competes with the subscription, its earning routes break store policy or reward activity that isn't learning, and time-limited rentals work against how students should review.

What to keep

KeepPay-as-you-go access for families who can't commit to an annual offer

The prototype's annual offers are priced in the thousands of dinars in the seed data. A cheaper, chapter-sized way in is a real need in Tunisia.

KeepReferral, and a Library that sells physical study material

Word of mouth between classmates is the strongest acquisition channel in this market. The Library also fits naturally with Wael Documents' printed material, which already appears in the offers as "هدايا من Wael Documents".

Critiques

C1The free path competes with the paid offer, and coins have no clear job

If every material has a coin price and coins can be earned through ads and tasks, then a patient student can rebuild the subscription for free. Before any pricing, someone has to answer: what can a non-subscriber reach in a school year by earning only? If the answer is "most of it", the subscription is cannibalised. If it is "almost nothing", the coins feel like a trick.

C2Time-limited access works against spaced review

Spaced practice beats cramming, and the best gap between sessions grows with how long you need to remember[3]. A bac student needs to come back to a chapter weeks later and again before the exam. A 7-day rental pushes students to cram inside the window and then locks them out at exactly the moment they should review. Separately, a PDF that has been downloaded cannot be "expired" without DRM, so time limits on documents can't be enforced.

C3Pricing per file puts a purchase decision in front of every act of study

"Is this video worth 15 coins?" is friction at the worst moment. Teenagers who are afraid of wasting coins tend to hoard them and study less, not more.

C4"Rate the app on the stores for coins" breaks store policy and can't be verified

Google Play forbids incentivised ratings and reviews, and violating apps can be removed from top lists or from the store[22]. Apple requires its system review prompt (5.6.1), which by design never tells the app whether the user actually rated, and it bans manipulating reviews (5.6.3)[20]. This route should be dropped permanently.

C5The ad route mints coins with no ad revenue, at the cost of study time

The ads are the platform's own promotions, so every coin they create is content given away. That is acceptable as a marketing funnel, but three things follow:

  • Time spent watching ads is time not spent learning.
  • Rewarding minors for watching promotions of paid offers is a form of pressure on parents.
  • Ads must never interrupt an exercise, a video or an exam.

Apple does allow rewarding users for watching an ad (3.2.2(x))[20]. If third-party ad networks are ever added, Google Play's Families ad-SDK rules and Apple's kids rules apply[23][20].

C6Buying coins with real money changes everything (open question)

If coins can be bought, in-app purchase is mandatory for digital content on iOS (3.1.1) and Google Play Billing on Android[20][21]. Credits bought through in-app purchase may not expire on iOS, and the stores take a 15–30% commission[27]. Apple also explicitly bans the usual local workaround of codes or scratch cards that unlock content inside the app ("license keys… QR codes", 3.1.1).

On top of that, many Tunisian bank cards only work in dinars and on domestic sites[26], so store billing may be out of reach for a large share of families. The same rules already apply to the existing offers if they are ever sold inside the iOS app (3.1.3(b)).

C7Coins that buy physical goods become a cash-like liability and a magnet for fraud

Once coins from ads and referrals turn into real books delivered to the door, farming coins pays out in real goods. Expect multiple accounts on siblings' phones, emulators and scripted ad views. It is also the strongest form of the overjustification problem: tangible, expected rewards for studying[8]. On top of that, Apple requires non-IAP payment for physical goods (3.1.3(e))[20], so coins bought with in-app purchase should not pay for physical goods.

C8Coins for completing a profile or answering surveys means collecting minors' data in exchange for currency

Tunisian law requires express, written consent (art. 27). For children it requires guardian consent and authorisation from the family judge (art. 28)[24]. Paying children to hand over data is the opposite of collecting only what you need. Surveys must be first-party and limited to product research.

C9The economy itself isn't designed

There is no model of how coins enter (sources) and leave (sinks), no caps and no admin monitoring. Without sinks, coin balances grow until either prices inflate or content becomes effectively free.

C10Cash on delivery is necessary in Tunisia and costly

More than 80% of Tunisian online transactions are paid cash on delivery[26]. The usual costs are refused parcels, delivery partners and stock. A minor placing a cash-on-delivery order that a parent refuses at the door is a predictable failure.

Propositions

P1Give coins one job: the "try before you subscribe" currency

The subscription unlocks everything. Coins unlock a whole chapter (lessons, documents, the practice set) for a non-subscriber, or an extra for a subscriber, such as an additional mock exam or a discount on a printed booklet. Make the value ladder explicit: free sample → one chapter with coins → whole subject or year with an offer.

Why it helps: One clear path to upgrade and no decision per file. A chapter-sized unlock matches how learning is organised.

P2Keep coins earned-only in version 1, with no real-money top-up

Why it helps: No in-app purchase, commission or card problem, no monetary value that makes the system feel like gambling, and room to tune the economy with real data before money is involved. Revisit once the data exists, and if the economy is opened to money, do it on web first, where local payment options apply.

P3An unlock lasts until the end of the school year (or until the bac), not a number of days

Why it helps: Students can come back for spaced review before exams[3], it removes the cramming window, and it avoids the unenforceable "expiring PDF".

P4Rebuild the earning routes around verified learning, with caps

  • Main route: learning milestones, such as a chapter mastered or a weekly plan completed. Pay out per milestone, not per action, and cap it per week.
  • Small route: the platform's own ads, at natural pauses only, capped per day and never during a study flow.
  • Profile: ask only for fields the product actually uses (level, section, subjects), and never pay for data.
  • Drop store ratings for coins completely.
Why it helps: The only reliable way to get coins is to learn. Milestone-level rewards are less controlling than paying per task, which lessens the undermining effect on motivation[8].

P5Firewalls: points never convert to coins, coins can't be transferred, nothing random

Why it helps: This prevents a black market of coins traded between students, stops farmed progress from turning into money, and keeps the product away from anything that looks like gambling[14].

P6Build the economy spreadsheet before writing code

Model weekly coin earnings for three typical students (the diligent one, the casual one, the farmer) against prices. Add an admin view of coins created and spent per week, plus a switch to pause any earning route.

Why it helps: Inflation and abuse show up as numbers before they show up as complaints.

P7Start the Library small: a curated catalogue, cash on delivery, and a parent in the loop

Start with Wael Documents printed booklets and bac preparation books. Orders from minors trigger a confirmation call or SMS to the parent's phone. Coins give a capped discount (for example up to 20%) rather than paying the full price. Pilot in one region before going national.

Why it helps: Cash on delivery is expected in Tunisia[26]. Parent confirmation cuts refused parcels and fraud, and capping coins limits the liability. Printed booklets of the very chapters students study online reinforce the learning loop rather than distracting from it.

P8Pay referral rewards on activation, not on sign-up

Reward both sides once the invited student completes a first week of practice, or subscribes. Add phone verification, cap rewards per month, and flag clusters of accounts on the same device.

Why it helps: Rewards follow real users rather than throwaway accounts, and the invited friend arrives already studying.

2Gamification town & league

Good but can be enhanced

Points earned from learning and buildings tied to subjects are a genuinely good idea. What makes Clash of Clans compelling (timers, scarcity, raids, paying to speed up) is exactly what must not be copied, and a global league would demotivate most students.

Critiques

C1Points from "activity" can be farmed

Video minutes (muted, background tab, phone left playing), PDF downloads and idle attendance in sessions all pay for presence rather than learning. Watching at 2× is not the problem: comprehension holds up to 2×[7], so penalising speed would punish efficient learners. Exploiting a system's rules instead of learning ("gaming the system") is the off-task behaviour most strongly linked to reduced learning[12].

C2Easy questions can be farmed too

Without diminishing returns, the best strategy is to repeat the same easy quiz. That is the opposite of desirable difficulty: the effortful retrieval and interleaving that make learning stick[2][4].

C3The pull of Clash of Clans comes from mechanics that are harmful here

Build timers, paying to speed up, raids that take your resources, and buildings that decay. These run on loss aversion and scarcity. They create urgency, and they push teenagers toward checking the app compulsively and spending, not toward studying.

C4The town can turn into a game that has nothing to do with the content

Time spent arranging and decorating buildings is not learning time. If the town becomes a destination rather than a ten-second glance at progress, it competes with study.

C5A global league puts almost everyone in a losing position

In a national ranking, about 95% of students are "behind". Relatedness through leaderboards does not predict enjoyment, and leaderboards demotivate the students at the bottom[11][10]. Bac anxiety is already high in Tunisia, and a public national ranking of minors by name adds social-comparison pressure and a privacy problem.

C6Badges and tiers lose their value if they're everywhere

"A badge or trophy means little without a challenge behind it." That line is already quoted in the repo's own research (Octalysis).

Propositions

P1The town is the student's map of mastery

Each building is a subject. Each floor is a chapter brought to mastered (passing a retrieval check) and then re-checked (passing it again a few days later). The town grows only from the mastery state in P2 of section A, so it can't be bought, farmed or decorated.

Why it helps: Progress is never fake, spaced review is built into how the town grows, and buildings tied to subjects make a neglected subject visible. "Your physics tower is two floors behind" works as a diagnosis, not a reproach.

P2A points recipe that rewards evidence of learning

  • Points for a correct first attempt, weighted by the question's difficulty. Retries within minutes earn nothing.
  • Bonus points for getting a previously missed question right after at least two days. This is spacing, rewarded[3].
  • Video points only through check questions inside the video. Short tests between video segments reduce mind-wandering and improve learning[6].
  • No points for downloads, for minutes watched, or for simply being present in a session (attendance is shown, not paid).
  • Daily caps per type of activity, and diminishing returns on the same question set.
Why it helps: The cheapest way to earn points becomes the way that teaches the most: retrieval, spacing and feedback[1][5].

P3Small cohort leagues instead of one global ladder

Groups of about 20–30 students at the same level and section, reset weekly, with promotion based on improvement as well as rank. Students appear under a nickname and avatar only. Leagues are opt-in for younger students, with no demotion theatre. Add cooperative goals for groups and classes.

Why it helps: Everyone has a realistic chance of winning something, comparisons are against peers rather than the whole country, and combining collaboration with competition is the most promising mix for motivation in the meta-analysis[9].

P4Flexible weekly goals instead of streaks

For example "4 study days this week", with rest days allowed and no message saying "you lost your streak".

Why it helps: Students keep a rhythm without anxiety, and it keeps the existing brief's "no streak, no nagging" commitment.

P5Few badges, each for a real milestone

First chapter mastered, first full mock exam, 50 reviews completed, a subject finished. Tiers (bronze, silver, gold) can be tied to mastered chapters rather than raw points.

Why it helps: Every badge stands for something the student would be proud to tell a parent.

P6Leave the timers, raids, decay and pay-to-speed out of the build list

Why it helps: They make a town feel compelling only by exploiting loss aversion. Without them, the town has to earn its place through meaning, which P1 provides.

3Community, rooms & events

Good but can be enhanced

Live group series are retrieval practice with immediate feedback, which is both valuable and engaging. But open chat between minors, scoring by speed, coin prizes and empty rooms are problems the plan doesn't address.

What to keep

KeepSynchronised series: everyone answers, sees the correct answer, continues, and gets a final ranking

This is group retrieval practice with feedback at every step, a strong learning ingredient wrapped in a social format teenagers enjoy.

KeepEvents launched by admin

Scheduled moments give the year a rhythm, for example a revision tournament before the bac mock exams.

Critiques

C1Chat between minors is a safety and moderation project

Apple requires any app with user-generated content to filter objectionable material, offer reporting with timely responses, let users block others, and publish contact details (1.2)[20]. Filtering Tunisian derja, Arabizi (Arabic written in Latin letters) and French mixed together is hard, and the risks of grooming and bullying are real. Chat logs are also children's personal data under Law 2004-63[24].

C2Speed-based scoring rewards guessing

A Kahoot-style "fastest correct answer wins" punishes students who think carefully and weaker students. Revealing in public who got it wrong can embarrass. Both work against learning.

C3Empty rooms

With a modest active user base, rooms created on the fly will sit at "waiting for players…", and an empty room teaches users not to come back.

C4Coin prizes invite cheating and collusion

Group members can share answers on WhatsApp, run multiple accounts, or use bots. If an event ever charged an entry fee in coins and paid out prizes in coins, and coins could be bought, that would be gambling-like.

C5"Read a course together" is vague

Reading in sync adds little on its own. The learning value comes from discussing, explaining and answering together.

C6Realtime is costly and fragile on mobile networks

It needs presence, reconnection handling, answer checking on the server, and load at event peaks. It is feasible with a managed realtime service, but it is ongoing operational work, and a foreign vendor brings in the data-transfer rule (art. 52)[24].

Propositions

P1Start with live drills hosted by the teacher, attached to existing live sessions

"After the session, a 10-question live series." Attendance is guaranteed, the teacher moderates, and the content matches what was just taught. Then add scheduled events. Rooms created by students come last.

Why it helps: It solves empty rooms and moderation at once, and retrieval right after teaching consolidates the session.

P2Scoring based on thinking, peer-instruction style

Correctness first, with a small capped bonus for speed and a minimum "think time" before answers lock. On hard questions: answer, see the class distribution, discuss for 60 seconds, then answer again. Show the distribution, never the names of students who were wrong. Personal results stay private.

Why it helps: Peer instruction measurably improves conceptual understanding[17], and it rewards reasoning rather than fast fingers.

P3A step-by-step chat policy

  • Version 1: quick-chat only (preset derja phrases and reactions), with no free text, no private messages and no images.
  • Version 2: free text for older students, only in groups joined by invite code, with a filter, report, block and mute, an admin moderation queue, teacher visibility, and a parental switch.
Why it helps: Most of the social fun comes with almost none of the risk, and it meets Apple 1.2 from the first release.

P4Fair events

Entry is always free. Prizes are titles, badges and modest coins. Question order is randomised, each question has its own timer, answers are checked on the server, and suspicious device or account patterns are flagged.

Why it helps: The excitement stays, the incentive to cheat drops, and there is no exposure to gambling rules.

P5Groups as study circles

Each group has a shared weekly goal and a joint progress bar (for example "our group mastered 12 chapters this week") alongside the challenges.

Why it helps: Belonging comes through cooperation rather than ranking, and pressure from friends points toward studying.

4Analytics, recommendation & planning

Good but can be enhanced

Measuring learning is the right foundation, and the planning feature is the most valuable idea in the plan. But vectors and segments are premature without tagged questions and enough data, popularity is a poor proxy for quality, and profiling minors has legal weight in Tunisia.

Critiques

C1Not enough data at the start

Collaborative filtering and student or content vectors need many interactions per item. At launch most items have very few, so the model will simply recommend whatever is already popular.

C2The indicators measure inputs, not learning

Study time, video minutes, PDFs downloaded and active days describe effort, and they are easy to inflate (section 2, C1). Diagnosing a level requires assessment questions mapped to skills, and the plan doesn't describe that tagging.

C3"Most interacted" is not "best"

Ranking by interaction favours long videos, and favours confusing videos that students have to rewatch. It also penalises teachers of hard subjects. The product data already models teacher pay rates and payouts, so if this ranking ever feeds pay or public "best teacher" lists, it creates perverse incentives.

C4Tracking every event of minors is profiling and has legal weight in Tunisia

Law 2004-63 requires a prior declaration to the INPDP for any processing (art. 7). For a child it requires the guardian's consent and the family judge's authorisation (art. 28), and INPDP authorisation for any transfer abroad (art. 52), with the request going to the family judge when the data concerns a child. That covers analytics, crash-reporting and AI vendors hosted outside Tunisia[24]. Breaching art. 28 is reported to carry up to two years' prison and a 10,000 DT fine (art. 87)[25]. Get Tunisian counsel before launch.

C5The planner could turn into nagging

Plans generated automatically, with red "overdue" badges and daily pushes, become guilt machines, which is the opposite of the "never nagging" principle.

Propositions

P1First, a catalogue of events and a tagged question bank

Use a small, stable list of events in the form "who did what to what, with what result" (an xAPI-like approach). Tag every question with chapter, skill, difficulty and correct answer.

Why it helps: This tagging is the asset every later feature needs: diagnosis, rooms, points, AI variants and reports.

P2Diagnosis version 1: simple, explainable rules

Base mastery per chapter on recent accuracy (the last N attempts, with older attempts counting less). Later, move to knowledge tracing, a standard model that estimates the chance a student has mastered each skill[15]. Always show the reason: "Recommended because you got 2/6 on Thalès last week."

Why it helps: It works from day one with little data, and students, parents and teachers can trust it.

P3Recommendation version 1: "the next three things to do"

(a) Reviews that are due (spacing), (b) the weakest chapter in the current school unit, (c) preparation for the next live session. Mix subjects within review sets.

Why it helps: It directly applies spacing and interleaving[3][4] without any machine learning. Use machine learning later, once the data volume justifies it.

P4Measure content quality with learning signals

How students do on the questions that follow a piece of content, where they drop off in a video, and a "this helped me" button. Show this privately to the teacher. Never rank by raw popularity, and never link it to pay without human review.

Why it helps: Teachers get feedback on what actually teaches, and there is no incentive to make long or confusing content.

P5The student owns the planner, and the app proposes

The student sets goals and the app suggests tasks. Tasks are phrased as concrete plans ("Tuesday after school, 20 minutes of physics review"), live sessions appear in the calendar automatically, missed tasks roll over quietly, and there is a short reflection each week.

Why it helps: Concrete if-then plans of this kind have a medium-to-large effect on reaching goals (meta-analysis d ≈ 0.65)[16]. Leaving control with the student protects motivation (the autonomy principle in the repo's design brief).

P6Privacy by design

Collect only what is needed, set retention periods, keep analytics pseudonymous, host data in Tunisia or with an INPDP-authorised transfer, and build a parental consent flow into onboarding.

Why it helps: Compliance becomes a built-in feature rather than a launch blocker, and it builds trust with parents.

5AI correction & generation

Good but can be enhanced

Feedback on handwritten work is the idea with the most learning value in the plan. Today's models still mis-grade handwritten maths often enough to hurt trust, Arabic and French notation is harder still, and photos of minors' work sent to foreign vendors need authorisation. Pilot it narrowly.

Critiques

C1Accuracy on handwritten maths is not good enough yet for unsupervised grading

GPT-4o grading handwritten university maths exams agreed with human graders significantly less than graders agree with each other, even when given a rubric. It sometimes marked clearly correct answers wrong and wrong answers correct[18]. On FERMAT, a benchmark of 2,200+ handwritten solutions from grades 7–12, the best model caught 77% of the errors, and models did better when the handwriting was replaced with printed text[19]. Telling a student a wrong answer is right is worse than giving no feedback.

C2Tunisian notation is harder than the benchmarks

Scientific subjects move from Arabic in basic education to French in secondary school, so pages mix right-to-left text with left-to-right formulas, and handwriting styles vary widely. The published benchmarks are mostly in English.

C3Cost, abuse and leaked answers

Every photo costs money for model inference. Students will also photograph homework to get the answers, which turns the feature into a cheating tool unless it gives hints rather than solutions.

C4Privacy of photos

Photos may show names, faces or the inside of a home. Sending them to a foreign AI vendor is a transfer abroad that needs authorisation (art. 52), with the family judge involved for children[24].

C5Agentic generation without review risks the brand

Generated exercises can contain wrong statements, wrong answers, off-syllabus content or the wrong difficulty. "Generated games" without a teaching design are entertainment. Unreviewed volume dilutes the reputation that Wael Documents' material has built.

Propositions

P1A narrow pilot grounded in a reference solution

Only for exercises from the Wael question bank, with the reference solution and a marking rubric given to the model (grounding in a rubric improved agreement with human graders[18]). Start with checking the final answer and key steps. The output is one of "looks right", "check step 3" or "not sure", and the model holds back when it isn't confident. Add an "ask a teacher" button, and have teachers audit a weekly sample. Publish the agreement rate internally before widening the pilot.

Why it helps: Students get fast feedback where it is reliable, and are sent to a human where it isn't. Feedback is one of the strongest influences on learning, provided it is accurate and about the task[5].

P2Hint first, solution second

Point to the first error and let the student retry. Show the worked solution after the retry, or when the student asks.

Why it helps: The productive struggle stays (desirable difficulty), and it is much harder to use the feature to copy homework.

P3Generation as a co-pilot for teachers

Generate variants of validated exercises (new numbers or context, same skill). Check numeric answers automatically by solving twice or with a computer algebra system. A teacher approves before anything is published.

Why it helps: The pool of practice questions grows fast, and retrieval practice and spacing need many fresh items[1], with quality protected by a human.

P4Handle photos with privacy in mind

Crop to the answer area, strip metadata, ask for no faces, keep photos only briefly, sign a data-processing agreement with the vendor, and get INPDP authorisation.

P5Measure the effect on learning, not usage

Do students who get AI feedback get the next similar item right more often? That is the number that justifies the cost.

7Recommended sequencing

Order by dependency, learning value and risk: the learning core first, visible progress second, social features third, the costly or risky parts as pilots. Each phase ships something students can feel.

Phase 0 · Foundations (before any feature)

  • Guardrail charter (section A, P3), and a decision on whether to retire the "no points or leagues" brief.
  • Legal review: INPDP declaration, a parental consent flow including the family-judge question in art. 28, and a hosting and vendor plan for art. 52.
  • Catalogue of events, and a tagging scheme for the question bank (chapter, skill, difficulty).
  • Economy spreadsheet for coins (only if coins are kept in phase 2).

Why first: these are cheap now and very expensive to retrofit. Every later phase depends on them.

Phase 1 · MVP: "practice that counts"

  • Quizzes and exercises in study mode, one question at a time, with immediate explained feedback. Timed exams and mock exams.
  • Mastery per chapter, and a review queue that brings missed questions back after a few days.
  • Check questions inside recorded videos.
  • Simple weekly planner (live sessions plus due reviews plus the weakest chapter), owned by the student.
  • Weekly digest for parents, and a chapter heatmap for teachers.

Why: it delivers the largest learning gain for the effort (retrieval, spacing, feedback), carries low compliance risk, and creates the data and question bank that every later feature needs.

Phase 2 · "Visible progress"

  • Points from verified learning, using the recipe in section 2, P2.
  • The town as a light mastery map, plus a few meaningful badges and flexible weekly goals.
  • Small weekly cohort leagues (opt-in for younger students), using nicknames only.
  • Earned-only coins that unlock a chapter for non-subscribers. The platform's own ads, capped and outside study flows.
  • Referral paid on activation.

Why: phase 1 gives the rewards something real to celebrate. Testing the economy without money is safe.

Phase 3 · "Together"

  • Live drills hosted by teachers after sessions, then scheduled events and tournaments with free entry.
  • Quick-chat only. Groups work as study circles with shared goals.

Why: the empty-room and moderation problems are solved by teachers and schedules before opening up rooms created by students.

Phase 4 · Pilots (measured, limited scope)

  • AI correction of handwritten work: one subject, one level, grounded in a reference solution, with teacher audit.
  • AI-generated exercise variants that teachers approve.
  • Wael Library: printed booklets, cash on delivery with parent confirmation, one region.

Why: costly, risky or operationally heavy. Only scale them once the pilot numbers (grading agreement, refused-parcel rate, cost per student) justify it.

Deferred, to revisit with data

  • Buying coins with real money (store billing, cards, commission, gambling optics).
  • Recommendation by machine learning, student vectors and segmentation (needs data volume).
  • Free-text chat, and open rooms created by students.
  • A global national league. Agentic "game" generation.
  • Dropped permanently: coins for store ratings, random rewards, paid entry to competitions, time-limited (days) access to content.

8Open questions for the client

Economy

  1. Does "charge coins" mean buying them with real money? On which platforms (web, iOS, Android)?
  2. Who are coins for: non-subscribers, subscribers, or both? What stays subscription-only?
  3. What does a coin unlock (a file, a chapter or a subject), and for how long?
  4. Can coins expire, be gifted or be transferred between students?
  5. What is the target: how many coins should a diligent student earn per month, and what should that buy?

Product

  1. Is the current "no XP, points, leagues or streaks" principle retired? Which of its guardrails stay?
  2. Age range: from 6th year primary (about 11) to the bac? Are there different social, ads and league rules by age band?
  3. How should points per subject handle subjects that have fewer exercises?
  4. Should the town be a quick glance (a mastery map) or a place to spend time?
  5. Will analytics ever affect teacher pay or a public ranking of teachers?

Compliance

  1. Has the INPDP declaration been filed? Who is the Tunisian legal counsel?
  2. How is parental consent captured, and how is the family-judge requirement in art. 28 handled?
  3. Where is data hosted? Which foreign vendors (analytics, realtime, AI, crash reporting) will need art. 52 authorisation?
  4. Which guardrails on admin-configured ads are hard-coded and can't be overridden?

Operations

  1. Who moderates chat and rooms (staff or teachers), and how quickly must reports be handled?
  2. Library: run in-house or with a partner? Stock, delivery company, returns, coverage? Must a parent confirm orders from minors?
  3. AI: which subject and level first, what error rate is acceptable, and what is the monthly budget per student?
  4. Do teachers have the time and incentive to host drills and review AI content? How are they paid for it?
