Can we Improve Tutorials for Complex Games?

Game Maker's Toolkit · 21:26 · 31/05/2021 · 8 moments

Mark Brown looks at why tutorials for complex games lose players and borrows fixes from simpler genres. The main ideas: spread teaching through play instead of front-loading it, start with one decision and a near-empty UI, and replace 'click here' arrows with tiny problems the player solves. He adds advisor characters for feedback and leans on conventions players already know. The same principles apply to any app that must teach a new user something without feeling like school.

  • ▶ 01:45

    A player's willingness to learn grows with their investment (George Fan, Plants vs Zombies). Lessons dumped at the start exceed that willingness; the same lessons accepted later feel natural. So let people play first and teach once they care.

    “a player's willingness to learn grows along with their level of investment”

    A simple graph: 'willingness to learn' on the vertical axis rising linearly with 'investment (time)' on the horizontal axis.

  • ▶ 02:54

    Replace the up-front tutorial block with small lessons sprinkled through real play. The player is in the actual game almost immediately, and each lesson arrives exactly when it becomes relevant (the crafting tip when you find the crafting table).

    “it can be split up and sprinkled throughout the entire experience”

    Two blocks side by side, a red 'tutorial' block before a blue 'gameplay' block: the old front-loaded model that the video argues against splitting up.

  • ▶ 04:01

    The 'inverted pyramid of decision making' (Civilization): turn one has a single decision, turn two has one more, and choices branch out only as the player grows. Start a new user with exactly one choice and let complexity unfold.

    “you basically have one decision to make on your first turn”

    A tree diagram growing upward from a single node into branches: one decision becoming two, then many.

  • ▶ 05:25

    Progressive disclosure of the interface itself. Mini Metro starts with almost no UI, just stations to connect. The line menu, clock and passenger counter appear only once you are playing. Ask of every panel: does it need to be there in the first minute?

    “At the beginning, there's almost no interface at all”

    Mini Metro early game: a white map with a river, three station shapes and two coloured lines, with only a tiny clock in the corner and no other HUD.

  • ▶ 10:47

    Blindly following 'tap here' arrows teaches nothing. The game counts progress, but the brain doesn't learn. Threes turns its tutorial into tiny puzzles with a goal ('use the walls to add 1 and 2') and lets the player work out the move, which is real thinking from the first second.

    “as far as my brain is concerned, I've learned nothing”

    The Threes board: a 4x4 grid of number tiles (blue 1s, a red 2, white 3s, 6s, 12 and 24) mid-tutorial.

  • ▶ 12:34

    Give objectives, not instructions, and pair them with an advisor character. Offworld Trading Company lists goals the player solves alone (no arrows) while a character pops in to explain or warn when something goes wrong. The guide supplies fast feedback and the 'why' behind actions.

    “use advisor characters who can warn you if you're doing something dumb”

    Offworld game view: a checklist of objectives at top right, and an advisor character portrait at bottom right with a text panel explaining resources and a 'How do I make food?' reply option.

  • ▶ 16:44

    Use icons people already know from everyday apps. In Total War Troy playtests, an hourglass 'End turn' button (which most people read as 'loading') left one player stuck for 40 minutes on turn one. Swapping it for an arrow fixed it. Don't assume genre literacy, and playtest the first minutes.

    “don't assume your audience has played other games”

    Total War Troy's circular HUD with the hourglass end-turn button circled in yellow, sourced from the developers' UX article.

  • ▶ 17:42

    Show, don't tell. Into the Breach explains each weapon with a tiny looping animation of its effect on the grid instead of a paragraph, which is far faster to grasp. Cut words, avoid jargon, keep language consistent.

    “showing that little animation of them moving is a thousand times more effective”

    Into the Breach 'Artillery Mech' card: a short caption above a mini isometric animation showing the shot hitting the centre tile and pushing adjacent units, with a Continue button.

For Wael Academy

  • Put the 3-question game before almost everything else. Level and subject can be one tap each, or even inferred, and all explanations of the town, XP and streaks should wait until after the first win, when the student is invested.
  • Start the town with one choice and almost no HUD. After the first game, reveal only the building the student just 'unlocked' and add coins/XP/streak counters one at a time as they first become meaningful.
  • Teach by doing inside the game, not with tooltips: the first question itself shows how to answer (e.g. a pulsing answer card with Wael gesturing towards it), with no 'tap here' arrow overlay. Wael acts as the advisor who reacts to mistakes with a one-line why.
  • Prefer familiar icons and short animations over text. Show Wael's explanations as tiny animated demos, keep RTL Arabic copy minimal and jargon-free, and playtest the first 2 minutes with real Tunisian students.

I Studied 1,460 Onboarding Flows. Here's What I Found.

Mobbin · 10:06 · 16/04/2026 · 8 moments

Mobbin's designer analysed 1,460 onboarding flows from 986 apps. Her conclusion: length is not the problem; what matters is how fast the user feels the product's value (the 'aha moment') and whether the flow feels short. The best flows sell the outcome instead of listing features, let users try the core experience before signing up, show what their answers unlocked, and keep something moving on screen. Friction is placed deliberately: a custom screen before permission prompts, and sign-up split into small steps.

  • ▶ 00:46

    Good onboarding is a loop, not a checklist: sign up, set up, reach the aha moment (where you actually feel the value), then build a habit. Every screen should move the user towards the aha moment as fast as possible.

    “That's where you actually feel the product's value.”

    A minimal diagram: a dotted circle with Signup, Setup, Aha Moment and Habit at its four points.

  • ▶ 01:25

    The first screen should show the product in action with motion, not explain it with text. Runbuds opens on an animated running track with friends' avatars circling it, so you grasp the idea before reading anything.

    “you get a feel for what it does without reading a single word”

    Runbuds' red welcome screen: a stadium track with runner avatars and scores orbiting 'Create group challenges', and a single white Start button.

  • ▶ 01:33

    Let people try the core experience before asking for an account. Alma lets you score a meal (the 'Alma Score' ring with food categories) during onboarding, so the value is felt, not promised.

    “It lets you try the core experience before you sign up.”

    Alma onboarding screen 'Balance your nutrition with the Alma Score': a 95 score ring above food-group tiles (fruits, vegetables, whole grains) and a Continue button.

  • ▶ 04:43

    Make onboarding questions use the product's own interaction. Speak (a language app) asks its questions in a chat bubble from its character and has you answer by speaking, so setup already rehearses the core activity. A quiet 'I can't speak now' escape keeps it pressure-free.

    “The steps before this screen already had you speaking instead of typing.”

    Speak screen: the blue character avatar asking 'What's motivating you to learn French?' in a bubble, a big round mic button at the bottom, and a small 'I can't speak now' link.

  • ▶ 05:47

    Duolingo has one of the longest flows (about 60 screens before sign-up) yet it doesn't feel long. Every step is quick and voiced by the mascot, and the first lesson plus its satisfaction come before account creation.

    “it doesn't even feel long”

    A Duolingo screen with only Duo, the green owl, in the centre: a single, uncluttered beat of the flow carried by the character.

  • ▶ 06:14

    Delight hides length. Bump animates even the loading and verification states, so something is always happening on screen and the user never sits on a dead frame. Moments that rarely get design attention are where the feel is won.

    “Even the loading states are wild.”

    Bump's vivid blue map screen with friends' avatars around a glossy 3D logo and a 'Get me in' button: a playful, game-like onboarding screen.

  • ▶ 08:04

    Before a system permission prompt, show a custom screen in the product's own voice explaining the benefit. This 'primer' noticeably raises accept rates; Center even previews the notification you would receive.

    “a lot of apps show a custom screen before the notification pop-up”

    Brilliant's primer: the mascot's bubble 'I'll remind you to learn so it becomes a long-term habit' above a mock iOS permission dialog with an arrow pointing at Allow.

  • ▶ 08:27

    More screens can mean less friction. Houzz split one long sign-up form into several one-question screens and conversions rose about 15%. Each small step feels effortless even though the total number of taps grows.

    “Maybe the friction we add in one place removes friction in another.”

    An A/B comparison: variant A, a single dense sign-up page; variant B, two simple Houzz screens with one field each, linked by an arrow.

For Wael Academy

  • Define Wael's aha moment as 'I just answered a real bac-style question and Wael celebrated it', and cut or merge anything between splash and that first question. Level and subject picks become two quick taps inside the town scene, not separate form pages.
  • Replace text explanations on the welcome step with motion: the 3D town comes alive (buildings light up for each subject, Wael waves) so a student understands 'this is a game world for my subjects' without reading.
  • Never show a dead frame between steps. Animate the hand-offs (camera glides from the town to a building for the subject, cards fly in for the questions, a 'Wael prépare ton défi' beat), so the flow feels like one continuous scene.
  • After the result, split account creation into one-field steps voiced by Wael with a progress bar, and put a Wael-voiced primer before any notification permission.

Diegetic UI - Realistic, or Distracting? - Extra Credits

Extra Credits · 7:54 · 14/08/2019 · 7 moments

Extra Credits defines the four kinds of game UI (non-diegetic, diegetic, spatial, meta) with a simple two-question chart, then looks back at the 2000s push to hide UI inside the world. The verdict: in-world UI is great when it stays as convenient and legible as a flat HUD, but cumbersome diegetic tricks draw attention to themselves and break the very immersion they were meant to create.

  • ▶ 02:02

    Classify every UI element with two questions: does it exist in the fiction, and does it sit in the 3D space? That yields non-diegetic (flat overlay), diegetic (in the world, characters see it), spatial (in the 3D space, only the player sees it) and meta (in the fiction, but on the screen).

    The Fagerholt and Lorentzon two-by-two chart with the four colour-coded quadrants NON-DIEGETIC, SPATIAL, META, DIEGETIC.

  • ▶ 02:11

    The same information can be diegetic or not: Halo 3 mounts the ammo readout on the gun, while a conventional HUD prints the number flat at the screen edge.

    A Master Chief doodle holding a rifle, with a flat '30' ammo counter floating at the bottom of the screen as the non-diegetic contrast.

  • ▶ 02:36

    Spatial UI (glowing arrows, an exclamation mark over a point of interest, a prompt next to the wall you can hide behind) is valuable because it delivers information in context, where the player is already looking, instead of a centred popup.

    A character beside a treasure chest with a big yellow exclamation mark floating over it, a world-anchored marker only the player can see.

  • ▶ 03:36

    Meta UI lives on the screen but belongs to the fiction, like blood splatter on the camera when hit: feedback felt as part of the experience rather than read from a bar.

    Red splatter covering a mock game title screen, the classic damage-feedback overlay.

  • ▶ 04:14

    Long in-world animations used as UI (opening a jacket to browse the inventory) are clever once and tiresome forever. Legible first, stylised second: in-world UI must never be slower or less convenient than the flat version.

    “good UI should be legible first and stylized second”

    The Alone in the Dark jacket gag: a character opening his coat lined with items like a street seller, the cumbersome diegetic inventory.

  • ▶ 05:27

    Diegetic UI works when it is as visible as a HUD would be: Dead Space's spine health bar succeeds because the over-the-shoulder camera always keeps it on screen (its 3D map, by contrast, was so awkward players abandoned it).

    Isaac from behind with arrows pointing to the glowing health bar on his back and an ammo count floating from the gun.

  • ▶ 06:50

    Irony of immersion: badly done in-world UI draws attention to itself and breaks immersion, while simple, concise UI is absorbed almost subconsciously and keeps focus on the play.

    The presenter swamped by panels, quest boxes, menus and prompts, illustrating how competing UI elements pull attention away from the game.

For Wael Academy

  • Classify every element of the onboarding with the four-quadrant chart: Wael and choice objects as diegetic (signposts, doors, books or buildings in the 3D town), the 'tap here' hints as spatial markers floating over those objects, the correct/wrong flash as meta feedback, and keep only a tiny non-diegetic layer (back, sound, skip).
  • Present level and subject choices as world objects (e.g. signposts or doors the camera flies past) but keep them as fast and legible as a button: one tap selects, labels stay large and readable in Arabic, no multi-step animation before the choice can be made.
  • Put hints in context: instead of an instruction banner at the top, Wael points or a small spatial arrow appears next to the object to tap, which suits a landscape phone where the eye is on the scene.
  • Avoid diegetic gimmicks that slow repeat use: the cinematic transitions should be skippable or shorten after the first run, so the in-world flow never becomes the 'jacket inventory' of the app.

Juice it or lose it - a talk by Martin Jonasson & Petri Purho

grapefrukt · 15:38 · 24/05/2012 · 7 moments

Two indie developers start from a flat grey Breakout clone and switch on one small effect at a time: colour, tweened entrances, squash and stretch, sound, particles, screen shake and finally eyes on the paddle. The point is that 'juice' is cascading, exaggerated feedback to minimal input, and that stacking many cheap effects turns a dead form-like screen into something that feels alive.

  • ▶ 00:36

    Juice is a catch-all for responses that wobble, bounce, squirt or make little noises: the game answers every small input with a cascade of reaction. A screen that only changes state silently reads as software, not as a game.

    “a juicy game feels alive in response to everything you do”

    The slide behind the speakers is a grid of easing curves, the toolkit they use to make every change move with character instead of snapping.

  • ▶ 02:15

    The baseline is a correct, fully playable game made of plain white rectangles on grey. It works but feels lifeless, exactly like a functional UI form: correctness is not what makes something feel like a game.

    The un-juiced Breakout: a block grid of identical white bars and a flat paddle, no colour, no motion, no personality.

  • ▶ 04:12

    Nothing should teleport. Even without a tweening library, moving a value a fixed fraction toward its target each frame gives a fast-then-settling motion. Then entrances get easing with overshoot, elastic bounce, rotation, scale and a small random delay per item so a grid assembles itself instead of appearing.

    “x += (target - x) * .1”

    The one-line easing formula on screen, the cheapest way to give any position, size or opacity change a natural ease-out.

  • ▶ 08:18

    Sound is the most cost-effective juice and the most neglected. The same animation of two circles reads as passing through each other when silent, and as colliding and bouncing once a hit sound plays on the exact frame of contact.

    The Steve Swink two-circles demo: identical motion shown twice, with and without a collision sound, to prove sound changes perceived physics.

  • ▶ 12:37

    Screen shake on impact makes the ball feel heavy and powerful for almost no effort, and a trail drawn from recent positions makes speed visible. Both are strong spices: dose them to the importance of the event.

    Mid-rally with a long yellow ball trail streaking across the screen while the camera shakes on each hit.

  • ▶ 14:30

    Giving an object eyes that blink, follow the ball, and a mouth that smiles on success or frowns when the ball gets away adds huge personality for tiny cost: the UI element becomes a character that reacts emotionally to what the player does.

    The paddle now wears big cartoon eyes looking toward the ball, the cheap character trick (added at 13:03) that turns a bar into a companion.

  • ▶ 15:00

    No single effect is impressive; the transformation comes from stacking dozens of small ones until every action triggers colour, motion, particles, sound and a reaction. Before/after comparison is the best test.

    The final all-effects-on state: ball trails, confetti and particles fill the screen and the eyed paddle reacts, contrasted with the grey baseline at 02:15.

For Wael Academy

  • Never let a choice screen appear statically: level and subject choices should assemble into the scene with eased, staggered entrances (slight overshoot, 40-80 ms random delay per item) and leave with a tween, so pick-level -> pick-subject feels like the camera moving, not a page swap.
  • Every tap on a choice gets a full juice stack on the same frame: squash on press, stretch/bounce on release, a short sound, a small particle puff in brand colours, and a reaction from Wael. A correct answer in the 3-question game earns stronger juice (bigger burst, light screen shake, Wael cheering) than a neutral selection.
  • Treat Wael as the 'eyes on the paddle': instead of sitting boxed in a side panel, he should live in the scene and react to the player's input (look at the hovered/pressed option, nod, smile on a correct answer, wince on a wrong one).
  • Run the before/after test from the talk on the current flow: record the welcome -> result screens with juice off and on; any screen that still looks like the grey baseline (white cards in a grid, static progress bar) needs colour, motion and sound added before it can sit next to the 3D splash.

Secrets of Game Feel and Juice

Game Maker's Toolkit · 5:19 · 17/02/2015 · 7 moments

Mark Brown contrasts a limp mobile platformer with games that 'pop and crackle', and argues game feel lives in the core second-to-second action: it should be fun even in an empty room. He then lists polish tricks borrowed from Vlambeer, Cactus and grapefrukt (screen shake, hit-stop, flashes, big sounds, rising pitch, smart camera, exaggerated scale, permanence) and closes with the rule that juice must amplify whatever the game is actually about.

  • ▶ 00:19

    A game can be complete and functional yet feel 'limp and lifeless': flat feedback, tiny effects, generic on-screen buttons. Players detect this within seconds of touching the controls, before any content matters.

    “the video game equivalent of a cabbage”

    Random Heroes on iPhone: a small hero, muted palette, generic arrow and A/B touch buttons and a pause icon, nothing reacting to input.

  • ▶ 00:33

    The same genre feels alive when every action produces energetic, oversized feedback: explosions, flashes, rings, numbers. Liveliness, not genre or content, separates a game from a flat screen.

    Super Time Force mid-action: a big yellow explosion, a timer ring and score HUD; the screen is crackling with feedback for one shot.

  • ▶ 02:50

WWDC23: Animate with springs | Apple

  • Steps = shared axis, RTL-aware

  • Persistent layers never pop

    The island backdrop, Wael and the progress path get their own transition layer (view-transition-name): they stay or glide to their new size and position while only the step content moves.

    from #JCJUPJ_zDQ4
  • The chosen thing becomes the next screen

    Container transform: the tapped subject building/badge grows into the game header; the result card grows into the account sheet. The eye never loses the object.
